§ 01 / Overview

The trade picture has flipped in 18 months.

India's solar trade is the single fastest-growing containerised commodity flow in or out of the country. The composition is shifting rapidly — finished modules from China have given way to a multi-product upstream BoM trade plus a high-margin westbound module export to the US.

§ 02 / Policy & Drivers

Every duty change moves billions of dollars of cargo.

Three policy levers — ALMM, BCD, and Anti-Dumping — explain almost every quarter-to-quarter movement in volumes and origin shares. Understanding the policy stack is non-negotiable for contracting capacity.

The ALMM Ladder

The Approved List of Models & Manufacturers progressively walks tariff protection upstream every 18–24 months. Each new list pulls a wave of front-loaded imports just before the deadline, then dampens that line permanently.

Mar 2023
ALMM I paused
Suspended for FY24 to ease module supply crunch — triggered the FY24 import surge.
Apr 2024
ALMM List-I reinstated
Mandates domestic modules for govt-tied projects. Module imports fell from $4.35B (FY24) → $2.15B (FY25).
Aug 2025
ALMM List-II issued
Domestic-cell list released. Triggered Q3-Q4 2025 cell pre-buying rush from China.
Jun 2026
List-II mandatory
All govt-tied projects must source cells from listed Indian makers only.
Late 2026
List-III (wafers) draft
Will mandate domestic wafers post-2028. Currently in draft.

§ 06 / Recommendations

A portfolio approach to lane contracting.

For a logistics company seeking 3-5 year carrier commitments, the strategically defensible plays. Don't contract a single-product, single-lane deal — secure capacity across at least 5 of the 7 priority lanes to absorb policy-driven volatility instead of being broken by it.

①
Anchor Position
Lock multi-year capacity on Shanghai/Ningbo → Mundra

10-15K TEU/yr on cells + wafers + BoM. The single highest-value lane. Buyer concentration (Adani, Waaree, Premier, Reliance) is favourable for back-to-back contracts.

②
Strategic Bet
Secure dedicated Class 9 hazmat capacity for BESS

Negotiate now with 1-2 carriers (Maersk, MSC are most likely) before that capacity tightens by 2027. Pipeline already exceeds 100 GWh — and only Class 9-capable vessels can move it.

③
High-Margin Export
Build a Mundra → Houston / Florida string for module exports

One major carrier partnership. Adani, Waaree and 4-5 other shippers feed it consistently. 40-50 day transit makes long-term capacity essential to avoid spot exposure.

④
Policy Hedge
Hedge against ADD volatility with ASEAN lanes

Don't put all capacity on China-origin contracts; secure ~25-30% on Vietnam, Malaysia, Thailand-origin lanes to absorb policy-driven re-routing.

⑤
Capacity Hedge
Add a Pipavav or Hazira string as Mundra-overflow

Mundra congestion is a real risk — backup-port capacity carries a premium worth paying, especially during Diwali and pre-ALMM-deadline surges.

⑥
Rate Discipline
For highest-tonnage flows, bid cubic-meter rate contracts

Solar glass, frames, low-value bulky BoM — this is high-volume, low-margin. Rate discipline matters more than slot guarantees.

Where long-term contracts don't work

Not every solar trade flow fits a contract. Three patterns disqualify a lane:

  • Episodic / project cargo (central inverters, manufacturing equipment, transformers) — volumes arrive in capex-cycle waves, not steady annual streams. Better handled as project-cargo bookings.
  • Volatile origin choice — if the shipper is still arbitraging China vs. Vietnam vs. Malaysia each quarter based on ADD developments, a single-origin contract can't keep up. Bilateral / multi-origin contracts add complexity.
  • Sub-scale buyers — below ~500-1,000 TEU per year per lane, contract administration costs outweigh rate savings. Better routed via consolidator / NVOCC at near-spot pricing.
  • Time-sensitive / one-off cargo — emergency replacement parts, sample modules, repair shipments — these need air freight or priority spot, not contract slots.

The contract-stacking play — back-to-back economics

The most efficient way to capture this opportunity is to lock in both sides of the transaction. Carrier-side: 3-year volume commitment to one or two ocean carriers. Shipper-side: 1-year rolling commitments from solar importers/exporters. The intermediating logistics provider captures the spread — and absorbs minimal mismatch risk if both contracts are index-linked to the same benchmark (Drewry WCI or Freightos FBX).

Why this structure works: If spot rates rise, both contracts adjust upward via index linkage — the intermediary's $300/FEU spread holds. If spot rates fall, both contracts fall together — spread still holds. The intermediary takes spread risk (small, hedgeable) instead of directional rate risk (large, unhedgeable).

Without the index linkage, this play is dangerous. The intermediary would be exposed to the 2022→2024 collapse pattern (rates fell 70%; anyone holding fixed carrier contracts at peak rates was crushed). With index linkage, that risk shifts to the carrier — who has the balance sheet to absorb it.

Why this is Adani Logistics' opportunity, not Maersk's or DHL's

Seventy percent of India's freight forwarding market is held by multinationals (DHL, DB Schenker, Kuehne+Nagel, Nippon Express). They have scale and global networks. But for solar — and especially for Mundra-routed solar — Adani Logistics has four structural advantages that no MNC forwarder can replicate:

  • Captive port at the heart of the trade. Mundra is already the #1 destination for India's cell, wafer and BoM imports — and the #1 origin for module exports to USA. APSEZ controls the berth, the THC pricing, and the dwell/demurrage clock.
  • Captive customer in Adani Solar. Mundra Solar PV Ltd + Mundra Solar Energy Ltd combined are India's largest solar module exporter and the single biggest user of Mundra outbound to USA. That alone is an anchor 4,000-7,000 TEU/yr book.
  • Owned hinterland infrastructure. 47 container trains, MMLPs, ICDs, bonded warehousing, FTWZ — ALL can offer port-to-factory-gate SLAs that pure forwarders cannot guarantee.
  • Existing carrier relationships. APSEZ already negotiates with every major carrier for berth allocation. Translating that into preferred slot rates for ALL-anchored cargo is a relationship layer the MNCs do not have.

Revision history — v3 (post sanity-check): Earlier drafts of this report (v1, v2) carried TEU estimates that were 30-50% too high on multiple lanes. The over-estimation came from three sources: (1) using 0.5-0.7 MW per 40HC for modules when industry-standard packing is 0.4-0.5 MW/FEU; (2) applying Mundra-specific lane labels to all-India totals (especially on module exports — Adani Solar's Mundra capacity is ~3-4 GW, not all of India's ~5.8 GW export volume); (3) overstating capture rates against MNC forwarders. Corrected estimates in this v3 are tighter and more conservative. Total ocean-freight TAM moved from $160M → ~$80-120M; EBIT estimate moved from $45-70M → $14-22M. The strategic logic is unchanged but the dollar magnitudes are now defensible.
