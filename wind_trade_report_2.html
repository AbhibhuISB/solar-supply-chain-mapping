Logistics Strategy Brief · India Wind Energy Supply Chain · 2025–2030

Where the Wind Goes.
And where it doesn't.

A field-level analysis of the highest-value cross-border trade routes feeding (and being fed by) India's wind energy boom — built for logistics operators evaluating long-term carrier contracts.

Geographic focus: India inbound & outbound Time horizon: 2025 actuals + 2026–2030 outlook Components covered: 8 major Routes ranked: 7

The headline: India is suddenly the second-largest wind market on Earth — and most of the value still crosses oceans.

In 2025 India connected 6.3 GW of new wind capacity — an 85% year-on-year jump — overtaking the United States and Germany to become the largest wind market outside mainland China. The country's wind manufacturing ecosystem has scaled to ~24 GW nameplate capacity (running at roughly 40–50% utilisation in 2025), and India is now net exporter of complete wind generating sets by a 19:1 ratio. But $650M–1.4B/yr of components — magnets, bearings, gearboxes, specialty resins, carbon fibre, balsa — still cross the border to feed Indian factories. Both inbound and outbound flows are now worth contracting for.

The shift in one paragraph

For a decade, India's wind supply chain looked like this: European technology, Chinese magnets, Indian assembly. That has flipped. Since 2023, Chinese OEMs (Envision, Sany, Goldwind) have undercut Western suppliers in India by an estimated 30%, while Indian players (Suzlon, Adani Wind, Inox) have moved up the value chain into blades, nacelles, and now main shafts. In 2025, Chinese firms supplied ~2.5 GW into India, domestic players supplied the largest share, and Western OEMs — once dominant — installed only ~1.3 GW.

For a logistics operator this matters because the component-level routing has decoupled from turbine-level branding. A Suzlon turbine still imports its bearings from Schweinfurt; a Vestas turbine still imports its blade core from Ecuador. The trade routes worth contracting are at the component level — not the OEM level.

Five strategic conclusions for a long-term contract

  • Anchor on JNPT + Mundra for imports; Chennai for TN-bound nacelles. Wind blade import data confirms JNPT (~52% by value) and Chennai (~26%) dominate. Mundra is dual-use: rising export hub for Adani, plus a growing import gateway for Gujarat-based projects.
  • Treat blades as breakbulk, magnets/electronics as TEU. A modern onshore blade is 78–91 metres — it cannot containerise. Carriers winning the blade trade are the project-cargo specialists (BBC, Jumbo, AAL, Spliethoff). Containers win the magnets, electronics, resins, and aftermarket spares.
  • The export leg is now real and growing. India exported ~3 GW of wind equipment in 2024 and is positioned to supply 10% of global demand by 2030. Mundra and Pipavav (private, deeper-draft) will absorb most outbound flows. This is the route where a 5-year contract can capture a structural growth curve, not just service existing demand.
  • The 5% concessional BCD window expires March 2028. The Union Budget 2026 extended the concessional 5% basic customs duty on bearings, gearboxes, yaw components, controllers, balsa wood and carbon fibre only until 31 March 2028. Carriers should structure contracts to either capture the front-loaded import surge before that, or transition to a post-2028 mix that will be more domestic-component intensive.
  • Offshore wind is a 2027+ inflection. India's offshore tenders have repeatedly stalled; the next 1 GW tender (split 500 MW Gujarat / 500 MW Tamil Nadu) is targeted for Feb–Jun 2026. Once that lands, monopile, jacket, and 12+ MW nacelle imports will require entirely different vessel types and likely a new dedicated heavy-lift contract — separate from existing onshore flows.

The 2025 inflection

India's 2024 was sluggish — only 3.42 GW added, dragged by transmission bottlenecks and delayed commissioning. 2025 was a step-change: 6.3 GW, an 85% jump. This was driven by (a) hybrid wind-solar-storage tenders that mandate a wind component, (b) the backlog from 2024 unblocking as transmission projects came online, and (c) Adani Khavda alone deploying 1+ GW of in-house turbines for a 30 GW renewable park.

BNEF expects ~5 GW in 2026 (slight dip, then re-acceleration), then steady 5–7 GW additions through 2030. Cumulative: +30 GW onshore by 2030, plus a separate offshore program targeting 37 GW by 2030 (though the first 1 GW slipped from 2025 to ~2026).

State-level concentration

2025 additions were highly concentrated:

  • Gujarat — 2.3 GW (top state; Khavda + Mundra hub)
  • Karnataka — 1.68 GW (legacy wind belt, Tumakuru-Chitradurga)
  • Madhya Pradesh — 703 MW
  • Tamil Nadu — Vestas/SGRE/Senvion factories; future offshore hub
  • Rajasthan, Andhra Pradesh — growing

For logistics: Gujarat ports (Mundra, Pipavav, Hazira, Kandla) handle ~45–50% of wind-component flows by volume, dominated by Mundra; Maharashtra's JNPT handles ~25–30% as the all-India container gateway (especially for declared HS 8503 wind parts); Tamil Nadu (Chennai, Ennore, Tuticorin) handles ~20–25%.

Localisation policy

The MNRE updated the Revised List of Models and Manufacturers (RLMM) in April 2025 to mandate that at least 64% of turbine component cost must be domestic, with an exemption permitting imports for up to 50 turbines or 200 MW (whichever is less) per manufacturer to ease new-entrant onboarding. Industry today already runs ~64% domestic content on average, and Suzlon claims 80–90% on its turbines.

The Union Budget for 2026 extended the concessional 5% basic customs duty on key wind components — specialised bearings, gearboxes, yaw components, controllers, balsa wood and carbon fibre — until 31 March 2028. This is the single most important policy signal for a logistics operator: a finite window in which the high-import mix continues before further substitution pressure kicks in.

In parallel, the government approved a ₹7,280 crore programme for rare-earth permanent magnet manufacturing targeting 6,000 tonnes/year — a direct play to reduce the China dependency that today supplies an estimated 60–70% of global rare-earth output. Until that programme delivers volume (realistically 2028+), China-to-India rare-earth magnet flows remain a structural anchor of the trade.

The export pivot

India is no longer just an import destination. In 2024 the industry exported ~3 GW of wind equipment; Suzlon's vice-chairman Girish Tanti has stated the sector can supply 10% of global demand by 2030. The drivers:

  • LM Wind Power (GE Vernova subsidiary) — exports ~70% of its India-made blades from factories in Dabaspet (Bangalore) and Vadodara to Europe, US, and Southeast Asia. 44,444+ blades produced in India to date.
  • Adani Wind / ANIL — first export shipment of anti-icing blades to Europe in 2025. 60 Hz design adaptation for US complete. Australia, Brazil, Philippines, Vietnam markets queued. Mundra is the export port.
  • Suzlon — ~6 GW installed outside India (cumulative). Recently certified its blade testing facility at BTCG (Vadodara) for the Dutch market (We4Ce).
  • Envision (Chinese OEM with Indian factories) — Trichy blade plant (2.5 GW/yr) supplies both India and export markets; 4.1 GW international order book in 2025, of which 3 GW destined for India but ~1 GW exportable.
  • Senvion India — 80%+ local content; Vadodara & Trichy blade plants, Baramati nacelle plant; exports growing as a strategic priority.

Estimated monthly TEU profile — wind component imports into India

Wind project commissioning in India follows a sharp annual cycle: pre-monsoon ramp-up (Jan–Mar) and a strong Q4 push to close fiscal-year financial commitments before March 31. The monsoon (June–September) suppresses construction, transport, and last-mile delivery. This translates to an inbound shipping curve with predictable seasonal peaks and troughs.

Why this is the anchor route

Why this is the verified anchor (not Germany alone)

Volza's HS 8503 shipment data revealed something the initial analysis missed: Denmark is the #1 origin of India's wind parts imports at 53% of shipments (Oct 2023–Sep 2024), with China at 31% and Germany at 8%. That makes Denmark + Germany combined the single largest declared parts origin (≈61%) outside China. The flow profile: Vestas's Danish supply chain (parts shipped to Indian assembly via Aarhus / Esbjerg), plus precision drivetrain from Germany — Schaeffler & SKF bearings (Schweinfurt), ZF Wind Power gearboxes, and Siemens Gamesa drivetrain components flowing into the Indian SGRE/TPG operation. A single string from a North European port complex (Aarhus / Esbjerg / Hamburg / Bremerhaven) covers both. Concessional 5% BCD on bearings & gearboxes until March 2028 keeps the import-substitution pressure off until then.

Why this needs a flexible contract

LM Wind Power exports ~70% of its India-made blades; Suzlon ships turbine kits globally; Adani is 60Hz-certified for the US. The Brazilian market added 2.2 GW in 2025 and is a meaningful Indian-supplied market. The caveat: the Trump administration's anti-wind stance has shaken US near-term demand — BNEF flags the US market as "on stake" in 2026. The right contract structure here is capacity option + volume floor rather than committed slots: lock in priority and base volume, leave upside flexible. Pipavav (deeper draft, less congested than Mundra for breakbulk) is the natural origin port; Mundra is the alternative.

Seasonality & congestion windows

When NOT to schedule critical components

  • Mid-June to late-September: India's southwest monsoon — Chennai, Tuticorin, JNPT all hit by heavy rain and cyclone risk; oversize handling capacity halves.
  • Chinese New Year (typically late Jan – mid Feb): Chinese factories shut; bookings out of Shanghai/Ningbo collapse for 2–3 weeks. Pre-CNY rush in early January pushes FCL rates up ~15–20%.
  • Diwali (late Oct – mid Nov): Indian customs clearance slows; truck availability for last-mile drops sharply. Avoid scheduling final-mile heavy-lift in this window.
  • March 31 / fiscal year close: Surge of last-minute commissioning shipments puts a premium on Q1 vessel slots. Worth pricing into a long contract.

Where capacity is structurally tight

  • Heavy-lift vessels in Indian Ocean basin: Wind blades increasingly compete with offshore oil & gas modules and refinery cargo. A 3–5 year COA today can lock in vessel days that may not be available on the spot market by 2027.
  • Flatrack containers ex-China: Carbon fibre, gearboxes and main shafts use flatracks. Supply has not kept up with demand; rates have risen ~25% in 18 months.
  • Last-mile oversize trucking in Gujarat & Karnataka: A wind project frequently requires 8–12 trucks per turbine for blades, tower sections, nacelle. Permit lead times can stretch to 4–6 weeks.

Customs & duty regime — critical context

The Indian import duty regime for wind components is currently structured around a concessional 5% basic customs duty on critical components, valid through 31 March 2028 (extended in the Union Budget 2026). The components covered:

  • Specialised wind turbine bearings (main, yaw, pitch)
  • Gearboxes for wind turbines
  • Yaw components and yaw drives
  • Wind turbine controllers / converters
  • Balsa wood (blade core)
  • Carbon fibre (blade structural reinforcement)

On top of this: anti-dumping duties of 5–15% apply to Chinese carbon fibre; Indian GST on imported wind components has been revised down (multiple media reports cite GST cuts on renewable equipment in 2024–2025); and FTA preferential rates apply to Japanese and South Korean origin (relevant for Toray carbon fibre and select bearings).

The MNRE's April 2025 RLMM amendment goes further: only components sourced from Indian manufacturing facilities will be accepted for inclusion in approved turbine models, with an exemption permitting imports for up to 50 turbines or 200 MW per manufacturer to ease new-entrant onboarding. This is the policy lever that will, over the 2026–2030 window, progressively compress import volumes except for the components where domestic capacity does not yet exist (rare-earth magnets, specialised resins, premium bearings, large gearboxes).

Risk, volatility, and where we estimated.

A long-term contract is only as good as the structural assumptions behind it. Here is what could go wrong, how we modelled the numbers, and where to challenge our estimates.

The five biggest risks to these trade flows

1 · China rare-earth weaponisation

The July–September 2025 Chinese rare-earth export halt (~3 months) cut global magnet supply sharply before being reversed in October. China holds ~60–70% of mining and an even larger share of magnet manufacturing. With each PMG wind turbine containing ~600 kg of NdFeB magnets, even brief Chinese export restrictions ripple instantly into Indian project commissioning. Mitigation: contract structures should include alternate-origin (Japan, US) ramp clauses; track India's ₹7,280 cr REE magnet programme as the multi-year escape valve.

2 · Localisation policy acceleration

The MNRE's April 2025 RLMM amendment, plus the Union Budget 2026 5% BCD sunset at March 2028, signal a policy intent to compress import volumes faster than the market is currently pricing. Mitigation: structure contracts to amortise economics before March 2028; build optionality to redeploy capacity to the export leg (Routes 06–07) post-2028.

3 · Offshore wind delay

India's offshore wind tenders have repeatedly failed to attract sufficient bidders (the 4 GW seabed lease for Tamil Nadu and 500 MW for Gulf of Khambhat were both cancelled in Aug 2025). The new 1 GW tender is targeted for Feb–Jun 2026 but the track record suggests further slippage is likely. Mitigation: do not underwrite vessel capacity to anticipated offshore monopile/jacket flows until firm tender award + financial close.

4 · US wind policy reversal

The Trump administration has implemented decisions explicitly against new wind capacity, threatening the export viability of Route 07 (India → US). BNEF flagged 2026 US market "on stake." Mitigation: contract Route 07 with low committed floor and high optionality; rebalance to Europe (Route 06) and Latin America during the US trough.

5 · Geopolitical / Red Sea

Houthi attacks in the Bab-el-Mandeb and southern Red Sea continue to push some EU–India shipping around the Cape of Good Hope, adding 10–14 days to Europe-India transit. Mitigation: contract pricing should include a Cape diversion clause; routes 03 and 04 are most exposed. Routes 01, 02, 05 (Asia origin) are unaffected.

6 · Domestic demand under-shoot

BNEF expects India's annual wind additions to decline slightly in 2026 (~5 GW vs 6.3 GW in 2025) before rebounding from 2027 as transmission capacity unlocks delayed projects. Any 2026–2027 import volume forecast should sensitise to a 15–25% downside scenario relative to 2025 levels.

The HS-code convergence — what we learned in revision

The initial version of this brief used a top-down estimate (annual wind capacity × cost-per-MW × import share) to size the import opportunity at $1.3–2.7B/yr. After the Indian DGFT formally defined the wind-component HS basket in October 2025 via the REEIMS notification, and after triangulating with two HS-level datasets that are publicly available (CEEW for whole-turbine sets, Volza for parts), we revised the figure down to $650M–1.4B/yr — roughly half. The route ranking and strategic case were unchanged; the absolute scale of the prize is smaller.

These share end-uses with EVs, aerospace, marine, and consumer goods, so customs cannot cleanly separate them from the HS code alone — which is exactly why REEIMS exists at all.

The two anchor datasets we found

CEEW — HS 8502.31 (FY 2023/24)

Council on Energy, Environment and Water, via Statista, published actual wind generator trade data for FY 2023/24:

  • Imports: $11.11M — whole turbines barely cross the border into India
  • Exports: $205M — India is a meaningful exporter at the turbine level
  • Net trade ratio: 19:1 in India's favour

This validates the "export pivot" narrative in Routes 06–07 with hard customs data, not just industry claims.

Volza — HS 8503 wind parts (Oct 2023 – Sep 2024)

Volza tracks shipment-level customs declarations. For wind turbine parts under HS 8503 in the trailing twelve months:

  • 3,236 shipments imported by 53 Indian buyers from 208 foreign exporters
  • Denmark: 53% of shipments (1,677 shipments) — the #1 origin
  • China: 31% (966 shipments)
  • Germany: 8% (253 shipments)
  • These three origins = 92% of total parts imports

This is what reshaped Route 03 from "German precision" to "Danish + German parts" — Vestas's Danish supply chain into Indian assembly turned out to be the largest declared European flow.

Why the original estimate was high

Two compounding errors in the top-down approach:

  • Equipment cost per MW was overstated. The $1.0–1.2M/MW figure is the full installed turbine cost, including Indian steel, civil works, transformer, cabling, OEM margin, and labour. The ex-works imported component portion is far smaller — closer to $0.3–0.5M/MW once Indian value-add is netted out.
  • Double-counting risk. Imported sub-components get assembled into Indian turbines, and the resulting turbines count toward Indian "domestic production." The top-down approach was effectively counting the same value twice (once at component import, once at installed turbine value). The HS-code-anchored bottom-up avoids this because it counts only what physically crosses the border under each declared HS code.

What remains uncertain even with HS-code anchoring: the end-use split within multi-purpose codes (HS 8483.40 is used in any industrial gearbox; only the wind share counts). REEIMS will fix this prospectively from November 2025 onwards, but pre-Nov 2025 customs data does not have this granularity. A 6–12 month REEIMS data extraction in mid-2026 will be the definitive validation.

How to validate these numbers

A logistics operator considering a multi-year COA should run these checks before committing:

  • Volza / Seair / Exim Trade Data subscription: these aggregators expose shipment-level Indian customs declarations (importer, HS code, port, value, quantity, country of origin). 6–12 months of pulled records will reconcile our estimates against actuals at the HS-code granularity.
  • DGCI&S monthly export-import data: at 8-digit HS code level for declared imports.
  • Direct conversations with OEM logistics heads: Suzlon, Adani Wind, Senvion, GE India, Vestas India, TPG-SGRE, Envision India, Inox Wind. Each will have firm 2026–2027 procurement and shipping plans.
  • Adani Ports (APSEZ) wind cargo data: Mundra and Pipavav are private; APSEZ can disclose handling volumes for wind cargo under NDA.
  • NIWE (National Institute of Wind Energy): for offshore wind tender timing and component specifications.

What changes if our estimates are 30% wrong

The two macro estimates that most affect the report's conclusions are (a) the total annual import value ($650M–1.4B post-revision), and (b) the per-port share. The first has roughly ±30% confidence given the HS-code anchoring; the second has ±30% confidence pending the first 6–12 months of REEIMS data. Even at the bottom of the revised range:

  • Route ranking stays the same. The order of Routes 01–07 by value is robust because the gaps between adjacent routes are larger than our error bars.
  • The anchor / Tier 1 / Tier 2 classification stays the same. Anchor routes (01, 02, 06) clear $250M+ even at the low end. Tier 2 (Route 05) stays Tier 2.
  • The export-leg structural argument is policy-driven, not volume-driven. Routes 06–07 are recommended for growth reasons (MNRE export push, OEM capacity build-out, 2030 target of 10% global market share), not just current volume — so even a lower-than-expected current volume does not weaken the case.
⚑ Caveat

This brief is a desk research synthesis, not a commissioned market study. We have triangulated public data, industry press, OEM disclosures, and freight industry data. Where exact figures are not publicly available — most notably monthly TEU splits by component and per-port — we have estimated and clearly flagged. A logistics operator committing serious capital should commission a primary research study (DGCI&S extraction + OEM interviews) before locking a multi-year COA. The analytical frame and route prioritisation in this brief, however, should hold.
